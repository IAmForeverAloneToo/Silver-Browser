Justified text

Justified paragraphs

A page is cut where the content no longer fits the space the page area leaves. Lines are never split across pages, so a break falls between two of them. The margins that meet an unforced break are dropped, and the content after it starts at the top of the next page.

A printed page has a box, its size on paper, and an area inside its margins where the document is drawn. Paragraphs flow from one page to the next as they flow down a long screen.

Each page shows a slice of the document, and the slices together show all of it. The reference browser prints at three hundred dots to the inch, and so does this engine.

The last line, and lines before forced breaks

A heading that ends a page alone is a poor heading, which is why authors avoid breaks after one. The last line goes to the right.

Orphans are the lines a paragraph leaves at the bottom of a page, and widows the lines it carries to the top of the next. The last line is centred.

A box that must not be broken is moved whole to the next page when it does not fit on the one it starts on. The last line is justified too.

Printing is the oldest way of showing a document, and the web came to it late; a forced break ends this line and the next line starts after it, justified only where it wraps before the paragraph ends alone.
